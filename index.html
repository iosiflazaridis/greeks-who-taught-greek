How the West went from graecum est, non legitur, "it is Greek; it cannot be read," to reading, teaching and printing Greek: the Greeks who taught it, the books they brought, copied and sold, the books bought, given or taken from the Greek world, the libraries that held them by 1600, and the first printed editions.

Overview

Medieval readers of Latin who met a Greek word in their texts could only write graecum est, non legitur and pass on. In the 1340s hardly anyone in Latin Europe could read Greek: Petrarch owned a Homer he could not read. By 1600 Greek was taught in the schools and universities of the West, 419 Greek texts had been printed for the first time, and its libraries held thousands of Greek manuscripts.

This page follows how that happened, from Barlaam of Calabria, who taught Petrarch Greek at Avignon in 1341–42, and Leontius Pilatus, who translated Homer into Latin for Petrarch and Boccaccio at Florence in 1360–62, to 1600: the Greeks who taught, copied, translated and edited Greek in the West; the books they brought, copied and sold, and the books that Westerners bought, were given or took in war from the Greek world; the libraries where those books were by 1600; and the first printed editions, with the manuscripts they were printed from.

It is a work in progress, released in numbered versions. A manuscript counts only if a source documents it in Latin Europe by 1600: 8,252 do, 6,744 of them with today's shelfmark, and 2,361 more are probable. Each book counts once. What we looked for and did not find is listed under How we count, with the sources that would settle it; corrections and sources from readers are welcome.

Before the 1340s: the prologue

The twelfth- and thirteenth-century translators and the first chairs of Greek, for context; they are not in the counts.

  • Council of Vienne (1311-12) Decree (canon 'Inter sollicitudines') ordering chairs of Greek, Hebrew, Arabic and 'Chaldean' at the Roman curia, Paris, Oxford, Bologna and Salamanca.
  • Burgundio of Pisa (c. 1110-1193) Pisan judge and envoy to Constantinople; Latin versions of Galen, John Chrysostom, John of Damascus and Nemesius.
  • James of Venice (fl. 1130s-1150s) Venetian cleric in Constantinople (1136); first Latin versions of Aristotle's Posterior Analytics, Physics, De anima and part of the Metaphysics.
  • Henricus Aristippus (d. c. 1162) Archdeacon of Catania in Norman Sicily; translated Plato's Meno and Phaedo (c. 1156) and brought a Ptolemy manuscript from Constantinople (1158).
  • Robert Grosseteste (c. 1170-1253) and his Greek helpers Bishop of Lincoln; translated the Nicomachean Ethics (c. 1246-47) and Pseudo-Dionysius with Greek-speaking helpers including Nicholas the Greek; John of Basingstoke brought Greek books from Athens.
  • Bartholomew of Messina (fl. 1258-1266) Translator at King Manfred's court in Sicily of Aristotelian and pseudo-Aristotelian works.
  • William of Moerbeke (c. 1215-1286) Flemish Dominican; Latin translations of Aristotle, Archimedes and Proclus; Latin archbishop of Corinth from 1278.
  • Nicholas-Nektarios of Otranto (d. 1235) Salentine Greek, abbot of San Nicola di Casole; interpreter and translator between Greek and Latin; the Casole library was later searched by Bessarion.
  • Maximos Planudes (c. 1260-c. 1305) Envoy of Andronikos II to Venice (1296-97); translated Latin works into Greek, the reverse direction.

How we count and how it was made

How we count

  • A manuscript counts if a source documents it in Latin Europe by 1600: an inventory or catalog made before 1600, an owner's note or arms before 1600, or a copy dated and placed in the West before 1600. "Latin Europe" is every territory under Latin bishops (all of Italy, Sicily included, after the Norman conquest; Spain; France; the German lands; England; Poland-Lithuania). Greek lands under Latin rule (Crete, Corfu, Cyprus, Rhodes, Chios, Naxos, Negroponte) stay the Greek world.
  • A manuscript that is likely but not documented by 1600 is "probable" and is not counted.
  • Each book counts once. Where an old inventory and a modern catalog describe the same library, the inventory entry is counted and the catalog gives it today's shelfmark when the two can be matched. A book from the catalog is added only if it cannot be one of the inventory's entries: it came after the inventory was made, or it belonged to a different collection.
    • Vatican: the inventory of 1475 (775 Greek books) is counted; 98 entries are matched to today's codices; 94 codices that came after 1475 are added; 450 codices of the old fund that were, or may have been, there in 1475 are not added, because they may be among the 677 entries still unidentified.
    • Venice: Bessarion's deed of 1468 (465 Greek entries) is counted; 106 entries are matched to Marciana shelfmarks; the 391 other codices of the Marciana's old fund are not added, as they may be among the deed's unidentified entries. A book recorded only in the 1474 inventory of his library, such as Venetus A, is added when a source identifies it.
    • Paris: Catherine de' Medici's inventory of 1589 (604 Greek books, Cardinal Ridolfi's library) is counted; 264 entries are matched to Paris shelfmarks; the 273 other Paris manuscripts marked as hers are not added.
  • Pinakes is not used.

The West

Iosif's ruling (3 November 2026): "We count all territories under Latin bishops as the West." So all of Italy is the West, Sicily, Calabria and the Salento included after the Norman conquest (c. 1071–1091), with Spain and Portugal, France, the German lands, England, Poland-Lithuania and the rest of Latin Europe. The Greek lands under Latin rule stay the Greek world: Venetian Crete, Corfu, Cyprus, Rhodes, Chios, Naxos and Negroponte. A book copied at Candia for a Venetian is therefore in group d, and a Greek who moved from Crete to Venice came to the West.

The groups

Each counted manuscript is put in one group by where and for whom it was written, as far as the catalogs say:

  • a From the Greek-speaking world: 2,208
  • b Copied in the West by a Greek scribe: 1,694
  • c Copied in the West by a Western scribe: 584
  • d Copied in the Greek world for a Western patron: 16
  • ? Not determinable: 3,750

A manuscript older than about 1400 with no Western place is taken to come from the Greek world (group a); a book copied in Italy is in group b or c by its scribe. Where the catalog gives neither date nor place, the group is not determinable.

The people

Greek speakers first: men and women from Constantinople and the Greek lands (Venetian Crete, Corfu and Cyprus included) and from the Greek-speaking south of Italy, who taught, copied, translated, edited, printed or bought Greek books in the West between the 1340s and 1600. 126 people: 119 on the core list, 5 borderline (among them Anna Notaras as a patron, Niccolò Leonico Tomeo, Galateo and Aemilius Portus) and 2 secondary (Demetrios Mysos and Camillo Zanetti). Leo Allatius and Georgios Koressios are left out: their work falls after 1600. Western pupils appear with their teachers.

The first editions

The first printed edition of each Greek text before 1600: 419 editions (texts written before 1453; grammars and liturgical books are marked as such). The list began from Wikipedia's list of editiones principes in Greek, checked edition by edition and enlarged; the Basel Nomocanon of 1561 is left out because it is in Latin only. Printer's copies are given where a study identifies them, above all Sicherl, Griechische Erstausgaben des Aldus Manutius (1997), and Martinelli Tempesta; 24 manuscripts of the list are linked to an edition printed from them.

The sources

For the manuscripts, the old inventories and the printed catalogs listed under By collection, all in the public domain or used for facts only: the Vatican inventory of 1475 (Müntz and Fabre), Bessarion's deed of 1468 (Omont), the Fontainebleau catalogs and Catherine de' Medici's inventory of 1589 (Omont), Pellicier's list, Orsini's library (de Nolhac), Janus Laskaris' purchases (Müller), the dated scribes of Vogel and Gardthausen, and the catalogs of some fifty libraries from Bandini (1764–70) to the Vatican catalogs of Devreesse, Giannelli and Lilla. From HathiTrust (copies downloaded by Iosif): Omont's Inventaire sommaire des manuscrits grecs de la Bibliothèque nationale (parts 1–3), Rocchi's Codices Cryptenses (Grottaferrata, 1883) and Iriarte's Madrid catalog (1769). For the people: the Dizionario Biografico degli Italiani, the specialist studies cited person by person, and Wikipedia. For the editions: Wikipedia's list, Sicherl, Martinelli Tempesta, library and incunabula catalogs. Every record keeps its own sources.

Pinakes is not used, by Iosif's decision (2 November 2026): the database of the IRHT does not allow its records to be reused without written consent.

The map

Land from Natural Earth (1:50m), in a conic projection. People's stations, collectors' acquisitions and editions are placed by the coordinates in the data. Manuscripts are placed with a small gazetteer of the place names that occur in the catalogs; names that are rare, unclear or offer a choice ("Venice or Rome", "Italy", "southern Italy", "not stated; the scribe worked in Venice and Rome") are left unplaced. Of the 8,252 counted manuscripts, 1,854 say where they were copied and 1,262 of these are placed (68%); 2,034 say where they came from and 2,021 are placed (99%); 8,238 (100%) are placed at the city of their collection. 474 of 482 stations of the people, 72 of 108 arrivals in the West (both ends named), 99 of 150 acquisitions and 419 of 419 editions are on the map.

Dates

A copy counts as dated when its colophon or a dated note gives the year: 1,090 counted manuscripts, 821 of them copied in the West. Dates given only by century, as "about", or inferred from a scribe's career are not used for the charts or the year slider. Journeys are dated by the year the source gives for the arrival, often a year "by" which the book was there.

The key manuscripts of modern editions

  • Suggested by @kirkaz01 on X: check the principal manuscripts that modern critical editions rest on. He pointed out that Venetus A of the Iliad (Marc. gr. 454) was shown as not identified.
  • 162 principal manuscripts checked, from Homer to the Greek Bible: 102 counted; 20 counted through an old inventory whose entry for them is not yet identified (Bessarion's 7, Catherine de' Medici's 7, the Vatican's 6); 11 probable; 16 with no evidence before 1600; 11 in the West only after 1600; 2 never in the West. They have a section of their own on the page and a download.
  • 8,252 counted (+20); 6,744 with today's shelfmark; 2,361 probable.
  • Venetus A is not in Bessarion's deed of 1468: it came to Venice after his death and is in the 1474 inventory of his books ("Homeri Ilias, in pergameno, pulchra"; S. Marcon, 2009). It now counts on its own. Venetus B is matched to the deed's entry 442 and Marc. gr. 456 to entry 443.
  • Counted on new evidence: the Ravenna Aristophanes (old Urbino inventory), Townley's Iliad (Burney 86), the Geneva Iliad (Henri Estienne), Triklinios' Aeschylus (Naples II F 31), Herodotus D (Vat. gr. 2369), Thucydides G (Munich gr. 228) and M (London Add. 11727), Euripides P and the only manuscript of Chariton (Antonio Corbinelli's books at the Badia), Hesychius (the Aldine printer's copy), Diodorus C (Vat. gr. 130), Erasmus' manuscript of Revelation (Augsburg), the Codex Leicestrensis and the Codex Montfortianus, the Cotton Genesis, the Codex Boernerianus and Archimedes' lost codex B; and the 1475 Vatican entries "Biblia" (the Codex Vaticanus) and "Libri Machabeorum", which our earlier reading of the inventory had skipped.
  • Identified: Vat. gr. 124 (Polybius A) with the 1475 entry "Polybius"; Vat. gr. 218 (Pappus) with no. 604 of the papal inventory of 1311.
  • Corrections: five pairs of Escorial shelfmarks that differ only in the case of the Greek letter (Υ.I.1 and υ.I.1, and four more) had each been taken for one book; Plato's Vat. gr. 1 had been confused with a copy of 1422; Hippocrates A (Paris. gr. 2253) had been counted through a misread list of copyists and is now probable; Paris. gr. 437 now counts as the Dionysius sent to Louis the Pious in 827; three old codices no longer carry the date of a later hand; modern dates for 23 principal manuscripts.
